Policy Perspective

On Institutional Constraints and Economic Tuning

"Change within an institution requires far more than just a change in leadership; it demands managing conflicting interests, navigating external rigidities, and mastering the fundamental operational practices."

— Yogeshwor Sharma

My engagement with economic theory began with an elementary question regarding monetary devaluation: why does the purchasing power of accumulated savings diminish over time? Early mentorship directed me to foundational macroeconomic mechanics—that price stability relies on managing the money supply. Yet, if economic policy were as straightforward as adjusting the pegs on a string instrument, why do persistent market distortions and unfeasible political promises continue to undermine fiscal stability?

This question led me to initiate an independent research inquiry interviewing former leaders of the Nepal Rastra Bank (Central Bank), National Planning Commission members, and senior commercial bankers. The investigation revealed that political parties frequently pledge unfeasible growth targets—such as projecting post-election growth rates outpacing major global economies—not out of a lack of technical calculation, but due to public opinion pressures and severe institutional constraints.

Later, as Managing Director of Financial Initiatives Nepal (FIN) and President of the Institute for Educational Accountability (IEA), these theoretical dynamics became operational reality. From navigating bureaucratic friction during registration to synchronizing diverse internal stakeholder timelines across rural and urban districts, I observed that policy failure rarely stems from ignorance alone. Rather, it is the result of unmanaged structural frictions. Effective governance requires institutional architects who understand both macroeconomic fundamentals and internal operational constraints.

Monograph Executive Summary

The Economic Feasibility of Political Parties’ Promises

Abstract & Research Methodology:

This paper investigates the divergence between election campaign promises and fiscal-monetary realities in Nepal. Through elite interviews conducted with former executive directors of the Nepal Rastra Bank (Central Bank), National Planning Commission specialists, and commercial bank leadership, the paper builds an analytical framework explaining how structural institutional limits lead to macroeconomic distortions.

Primary Conclusions:

  • Political parties routinely project growth targets exceeding historical capital expenditure capacities due to institutional signaling pressure.
  • Macroeconomic policy decisions require managing conflicting internal constraints rather than relying solely on theoretical models.
  • Institutional reform must establish independent vetting mechanisms for electoral promises to ensure inflation and debt stability.
